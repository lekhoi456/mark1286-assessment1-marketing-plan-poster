# Independent Examiner 3 — MARK1286 Assessment 1

## Decision

**Poster-only mark: 77/100. Overall confidence: moderate.**

The artefact presents a coherent, visually distinctive marketing plan with unusually clear links between customer acquisition, service delivery, retention and staged investment. It meets the substantive coverage of the brief. The principal limitations are incomplete justification of target selection and budget assumptions, limited execution detail behind several digital tactics and innovation pilots, and a layout that gives considerable space to illustration while making assessed detail small.

This is an independent evaluation of the supplied artefact, not an official module result and not an assessment of an unseen presentation. The seven criteria and weights below are taken directly from the supplied handbook. No additional numerical marking bands or automatic penalties have been invented.

## Evidence boundary and method

Read only the original assessment brief, the specified Assessment 1 passages of the module handbook, and `poster/poster-references-v02-2026-10-05.svg` with the images it references. Rendered that SVG at 6,688 pixels wide; inspected a complete overview, all ten content panels and the full reference strip at higher magnification. No previous version, project commentary, evidence register, copy master or review was used. No project artefact was modified.

References are assessed as visible references. Their underlying contents and the factual accuracy of corporate, competitor and survey claims were not independently checked because those sources are outside the permitted evidence set. A plausible-looking bibliography is not treated as proof of every claim.

### 1. Company Background and Country Context — 8/10

**Credit.** Panel 1 makes origin, background, service and operating model easy to identify: “Vingroup-backed”, “Founded March 2023”, Vietnam marked with a home symbol, “Owned fleet · Employed drivers” and “app-booked electric taxi rides in Copenhagen”. The country flags and Denmark launch date provide international context. Panel 3 names specific competitors and distinguishes ownership of the fleet and employment of drivers from the proposed taxi-partner comparison. This is more informative than a generic company introduction.

**Marks withheld.** Country context remains mainly geographic and competitive. The poster does not explain much about the local conditions that make this entry attractive or constrain the proposed operating model. The flags communicate presence but provide little business context beyond that. The title describes market entry while Panel 1 records an existing Danish launch: these are reconcilable as a subsequent 12-month entry-development plan, but the starting point is not explicit. No penalty is based on an allegation that the displayed launch dates are false.

### 2. Target Market, Segmentation & Branding — 12/15

**Credit.** Panel 2 explicitly combines geography and age (“Copenhagen residents aged 25–44”) with behavioural filters (“Self-paying”, “Existing taxi users”, “Recurring local trips”). It adds a labelled proposed psychographic profile: price consciousness, trip control and fair treatment. The 269,277 population figure and age chart make the demographic choice visible. Panel 3 states a clear proposition, “Electric rides. One managed experience”, and recognises that app booking and electric options are shared with competitors. Panel 4 supplies logo, colours, message, typography/interface direction and a useful localisation choice, “Danish first · English second”.

**Marks withheld.** The age group is selected largely through its population size; the poster does not show why it should convert or repeat more effectively than competing segments. The 269,277 figure is the age-defined population, not a quantified estimate of self-paying existing taxi users with recurring local trips. The proposed profile is appropriately labelled but not demonstrated. Branding is consistent with electric mobility, although the connection from specific audience motivations to the brand message is only partly explained. “Direct control → one service standard” is a plausible proposed advantage, not evidence that customers perceive superior reliability.

### 3. Digital Marketing & Sales Strategies — 15/20

**Credit.** Panels 5–6 give a practical customer journey: search/social discovery, fare checking, app booking, a completed first paid ride, experience checks, feedback and repeat use. The proposed 4Ps are visible. The local landing-page–app–ride/help sequence and “Same fares / offers / help” make omnichannel consistency concrete. The 10% versus 20% first-ride test is bounded by a DKK30 maximum, a 400 cap and one use per rider. The plan stages verification, testing and potential scaling across the year. Sample sizes are visibly distinguished between Denmark and the Capital Region, avoiding an unlabelled pooling of the two.

**Marks withheld.** “Search / SEO / Social” names channels but does not provide a worked keyword, social platform/content example, creative message or landing-page proposition. The survey statistics “40–50% one app” and “Lower price 57% / Usual app 22%” lack the question and denominator context needed to interpret the behaviour precisely from the poster alone. The voucher comparison does not visibly specify allocation between test groups or a full decision rule; “30-day paid” is compressed. Lead and prospect stages are drawn clearly but not quantified through to first ride. Repeat vouchers and monthly bundles remain separately costed pilot intentions. These limitations restrict the depth of practical execution rather than negate the coherent journey.

### 4. Budget & Resource Allocation — 12/15

**Credit.** Panel 7 fulfils the explicit visual-budget requirement with a readable bar presentation, currency, percentages, time period and VAT scope. The seven figures sum exactly to DKK600,000: 276,000 + 108,000 + 90,000 + 43,195 + 30,000 + 12,000 + 40,805. Displayed percentages sum to 100%. Team allocation is internally consistent: 60 + 120 + 160 + 120 = 460 hours, and 460 × DKK600 = DKK276,000. The first-ride voucher ceiling also reconciles: 400 × DKK30 = DKK12,000. The M6 commitment plus held balance reconciles to the total: DKK265,000 + DKK335,000 = DKK600,000. Explicit exclusions and staged reviews are strengths.

**Marks withheld.** The team rate is explicitly a “Planning allowance”, but the poster supplies little basis for its realism or for the sufficiency of 460 hours over 12 months. Most media and tool amounts are not tied to visible quantities, durations, unit prices or funnel assumptions. The screens amount is precise without a visible placement/package calculation. The bibliography includes a price list, but the reader cannot trace that number to a particular purchase. App/platform build and several proposed pilots are outside the total: this is honestly stated, yet it leaves part of the proposed strategy without an implementable resourcing envelope. Excluding vehicle and driver operating costs from a marketing budget is not itself treated as an error.

### 5. KPIs & Success Metrics Aligned with Objectives — 8/10

**Credit.** Panel 8 provides quantified, time-bound targets for consideration, distinct first paid riders, paid rides, 90-day repeat and acquisition cost. It marks the paid campaign cohort, separates organic/referral upside and specifies full 90-day cohorts. Service completion, cancellation and response-time thresholds support the reliability proposition. M4 and M6 checkpoints, weekly actions and monthly cohort review show how measures influence management. Panel 10 visibly connects these measures to local recognition, stable operation, retention and controlled expansion.

**Marks withheld.** The consideration measure lacks a visible sampling method or baseline value. The cost-per-first-paid-rider numerator is not explicitly defined: search plus social totals DKK198,000 and DKK198,000 / 1,200 = DKK165, suggesting a media-only measure, but the poster does not say this. It should not be read as an all-in acquisition cost. “Contribution” is used in Panel 10 as a growth/review criterion without a numeric threshold or definition. The scheduling implications of observing a full 90-day cohort at M12 are not specified. These are definition and decision-rule gaps, not missing KPIs.

### 6. Creativity & Use of Emerging Concepts — 7/10

**Credit.** Panel 9 turns emerging concepts into four identifiable use cases: consented-data targeting/reminders, AI dispatch, connected care using one trip ID, and personalised prepaid/monthly packages. It includes comparison with a local baseline, human hand-off and retention/margin checks. Panel 5 links CDP, data patterns and human-reviewed reminders. “PROPOSED PILOTS · Local performance untested” appropriately separates plans from established results. The taxi-shaped overall composition is original and closely related to the business.

**Marks withheld.** Several elements remain concept sketches rather than a specified local experiment. There is no clear targeting example, minimal data/input design, priced pilot scope or quantitative success threshold for the AI and connected-care work. “Unpriced dispatch/chatbot/subscription: cost separately” openly identifies this incompleteness. The innovation is mainly in combination and application of familiar concepts, with limited explanation of what is distinctive for the chosen Copenhagen segment. No marks are deducted merely for omitting neuromarketing: the rubric gives emerging concepts as examples, not a requirement to include every named trend.

### 7. Visual Appeal & Presentation Quality — 15/20

**Credit.** The coherent cyan/yellow identity, Copenhagen setting and integrated taxi form create a strong first impression. Numbered white panels provide a reasonably intuitive reading sequence. Repeated icon styles, diagrams, the demographic chart, budget bars and prominent KPI cards satisfy the rubric's emphasis on visual communication. All substantive brief elements are present: the student/business identity cloud plus ten content panels cover the eleven listed requirements. Student names and numbers are visible. The reference strip is a real, substantial bibliography, not absent referencing.

**Marks withheld.** At the whole-poster scale, the illustration occupies considerable space while execution conditions, footnotes and the bibliography become small. The strongest hierarchy belongs to the title and car rather than the plan's central commercial argument. Panels 5, 8, 9 and 10 require close reading, and the long reference URLs intensify the scale problem. There are no visible claim-level author/date citations or numbered source links in the content panels, leaving the bibliography hard to map to individual data and competitor claims. This is a traceability weakness, not a claim that there are no sources. The simulated hand-drawn appearance cannot establish the production method or participation of every group member.

## Compliance and matters not verifiable

- **Physical format:** the SVG declares 1,189 × 841 mm (A0 landscape). The supplied original brief specifies one A1 sheet; the supplied handbook says one standard-size paper chart without naming A1. The file therefore does not match the original brief's named size. Whether it will be reproduced on A1 or whether an alternative size was authorised is unknown. This is recorded as an unresolved compliance point; no invented automatic percentage deduction has been applied. Reduction to A1 would further reduce the physical size of already small text.
- **Hand-drawn requirement:** both supplied documents refer to a hand-drawn chart. A digital SVG with a hand-drawn visual style does not establish that a physical hand-drawn chart will be presented. The permitted evidence cannot resolve this.
- **Reference location:** the original brief asks for references at the end; the handbook asks for them on the back of the chart. The visible front/bottom reference strip satisfies an end-of-poster interpretation, but the actual reverse of a physical chart is unseen.
- **Group contribution and delivery:** names and numbers are visible; individual contribution and active participation cannot be verified. The brief qualifies contribution labels with “if applicable”. No fabricated account of contribution, speaking quality, timing, Q&A or rehearsal has been used, and none is assumed to have failed.
- **Submission:** Moodle submission, individual accounts, tutorial-room/time/tutor submission metadata and the eventual physical output are outside the evidence. The conflicting due dates in the two supplied documents are not a poster-content defect and are not used to reduce marks.

## Most consequential strengths and losses

1. **Strongest feature:** a joined-up acquisition–service–retention plan, with explicit measurement and staged budget release rather than disconnected marketing ideas.
2. **Main analytical loss:** population size is used more convincingly than segment attractiveness; local audience and performance assumptions remain lightly supported on the poster.
3. **Main implementation loss:** channel execution, media quantities and emerging-concept pilots need more specific, costed detail to warrant higher marks for practicality.
4. **Main communication loss:** a striking visual identity is achieved at the expense of reading size and direct claim-to-source traceability.

**Confidence:** high in visible coverage, arithmetic and the recorded layout observations; moderate in the exact 77/100 judgement; unknown for live delivery, group participation, submission and physical-production compliance.

**Unresolved questions:** final physical size and medium; any authorised variation from the original brief; live group delivery; the operational definitions of acquisition cost and contribution. These are unknowns, not assumed breaches.
